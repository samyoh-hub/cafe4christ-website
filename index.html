Our First Project

A café at Mustard Seed Church — Saitama, Japan

This wasn't where we planned to start — but Mustard Seed Church in Saitama reached out, asked if we could help them open a café for the neighborhood, and we said yes. That's how this began. And what we've seen since has only made us more sure.

What we're doing

Opening a café inside Mustard Seed Church

We're helping Mustard Seed Church in Saitama build and open a café inside their building — a real gathering place where the neighborhood can come in, get a good cup of coffee, and meet the people of the church naturally.

Why it matters

A front door the neighborhood will actually use

Most people in Japan won't walk into a church. A good café? They already do. This project gives Mustard Seed a front door the neighborhood will use — long before they'd ever step into a Sunday service.

Our role

Operations, structure, and ongoing support

We're not writing a check and walking away. We're in it — sourcing equipment, helping plan the space, and making sure the structure is there so the café can actually run well.

About Cafe for Christ

We coordinate.
We don't do it all.

Cafe for Christ is a ministry built around a simple idea: hospitality opens the door that argument cannot. We partner with local churches to open neighborhood cafés — practical, welcoming spaces that meet people where they already are.

We bring together café operators, pastoral teams, and an oversight structure — making sure each one can do their job without carrying someone else's. Role separation from day one. The café runs like a café. The church is led like a church.

Saitama, Japan is where this starts. Mustard Seed Church is our first partner, and this is our first step.

The Model

Three things this model gets right

First Barrier

The first step becomes smaller

In Japan, walking into a church is a significant social act. Walking into a café isn't. OMF Japan describes café ministry as a non-intimidating space where people engage with faith far more naturally than they would in a church.[6]

A café doesn't replace a church. It makes the first step possible.

Facility Costs

The rent becomes manageable

The average Japanese church has 35 members — faithful communities carrying real costs. A shared-space model changes that. The café runs daily and covers most of the lease, documented as roughly a 6-to-1 split by actual use.[8]

The church doesn't fund the café. The café makes the space viable for both.

Pastoral Capacity

The pastor stays a pastor

Running a café is a full-time job. Leading a church is a full-time job. The goal is to protect both — and that means dedicated support for each role from the start.

The pastor preaches, disciples, and cares for people.

The operator runs the café with independent support.

Both do their actual job. From day one.

Why Japan

The numbers tell the story

These aren't assumptions — they're documented. Every claim below is cited and linked.

71% of people in Japan have no personal religion and no family religion.[1] Christians are roughly 1% of the population.[2] Churches and missionaries have faithfully served Japan for generations. A café is one more way to open a door — meeting people in a space they already feel at home in.

81% of Protestant churches in Japan have fewer than 50 members.[5] 89% of pastors are over 50. The average congregation is about 35 people.[3][4] These are faithful communities doing significant work with limited resources. A shared space is one way to expand what's possible alongside them.

OMF Japan calls café ministry a "comfortable, low-pressure bridge" where people engage more naturally than in a church.[6] Japan also consumes over 400,000 metric tons of coffee a year — coffee shops are already where people spend real time.[9] The café meets people in a space they already trust.

Most churches show up once a week. This one shows up every day.

A café open five days a week builds something a Sunday service alone can't — familiarity, trust, and a reason to keep coming back. The barista knows your name. The corner table feels like yours. The space already belongs to you before anyone has asked you to believe anything.

When the church gathers in that same room, the regulars already know it. The first-time barrier is already gone — not because of a program, but because they already belong to the space.
