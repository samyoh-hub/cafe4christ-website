Our Vision

Community, coffee, purpose.

Cafe for Christ is a church plant built around a simple idea: hospitality opens the door that argument cannot. We want our community to feel like a warm, unhurried conversation over coffee — modern and unpretentious, welcoming to someone in their twenties and someone in their sixties alike, and quietly rooted in faith rather than announcing it.

We're a newly formed California nonprofit religious corporation, led by a three-person founding pastoral team. Chino, California is where our gatherings start — but the vision has always reached further than one room.

This is where it starts.

Our First Project

A café at Mustard Seed Saitama

Before Cafe for Christ opens a café of its own, we're standing behind someone else's. Our first project is a partnership with Mustard Seed Saitama, an existing church in Saitama, Japan — helping them open a café inside their own building as a tool for outreach.

It's not exactly where we started, but it's exactly the kind of thing we exist to do: give a faithful, under-resourced church a practical way to open its doors wider. We expect our vision here in California to keep growing alongside it.

Thanks to generous early support, tenant improvements, much of the build-out, and most of the equipment are already funded. What's still needed: coffee beans and ongoing supplies, staffing support, and travel support for short-term missionaries.

Why This Model Works

Three things this approach addresses

First barrier

The first step becomes smaller

In Japan, walking into a church is a significant social act. Walking into a café isn't. OMF Japan describes café ministry as a non-intimidating space where people engage with faith far more naturally than they would in a church.[6]

This has worked before. A coffee shop in Sapporo, starting in 1971, led directly to a church plant within the United Church of Christ in Japan.[7]

A café doesn't replace a church. It makes the first step possible.

Facility costs

The rent becomes manageable

The average Japanese church has 35 members — faithful communities carrying real costs. Paying for a space that sits empty six days a week is a structural challenge.

A shared-space model changes that. The café runs five to six days a week and covers most of the lease. The church uses the same space and pays its share — documented as roughly a 6-to-1 split by actual use.[8]

The church doesn't fund the café. The café makes the space viable for both.

Pastoral capacity

The pastor stays a pastor

Running a café is a full-time job. Leading a church is a full-time job. The goal is to protect both — and that means dedicated support for each role.

Exponential documented this clearly: café operations need their own dedicated support to sustain well.[8]

The pastor preaches, disciples, and cares for people.

The operator runs the café with independent support.

Both do their actual job. From day one.

Why It Works in Japan

The numbers behind our first project

These are documented conditions that shaped our decision to partner with Mustard Seed Saitama — not assumptions. Every claim below is cited and linked.

71% of people in Japan have no personal religion and no family religion.[1] Christians are roughly 1% of the population.[2] Churches and missionaries have faithfully served Japan for generations. A café is one more way to open a door — meeting people in a space they already feel at home in.

81% of Protestant churches in Japan have fewer than 50 members.[5] 89% of pastors are over 50. The average congregation is about 35 people.[3][4] Mustard Seed Saitama is one of these faithful communities doing significant work with limited resources. A shared space is one way to expand what's possible alongside them.

OMF Japan calls café ministry a comfortable, low-pressure bridge where people engage more naturally than in a church.[6] Japan also consumes over 400,000 metric tons of coffee a year — coffee shops are already where people spend real time.[9][10] The café meets people in a space they already trust.

Most churches show up once a week. This one shows up every day.

A café open five days a week builds something a Sunday-only rhythm alone can't — familiarity, trust, and a reason to keep coming back. The barista knows your name. The corner table feels like yours. The space already belongs to you before anyone has asked you to believe anything.

When the church gathers in that same room, the regulars already know it. The first-time barrier — the one that keeps most people in Japan from ever walking through a church door — is already gone. Not because of a program. Because they already belong to the space.

That's what we're hoping to help create with Mustard Seed Saitama. Not just a cheaper lease. A church woven into everyday life — present in the neighborhood five days a week, trusted before it's understood, and growing because of it.
